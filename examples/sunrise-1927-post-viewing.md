# *Sunrise: A Song of Two Humans* (1927), dir. F.W. Murnau — after viewing

Now that you've seen it, here's what's worth knowing.

**What you likely missed as a modern viewer.** The technical feats are nearly invisible to us because we're accustomed to seamless effects, but in 1927 they were astonishing. The tracking shot where The Man walks through the marsh to meet The Woman from the City is a complex multi-plane setup — the camera on an overhead trolley, the actor passing forced-perspective sets, the background dissolving from marsh to her waiting figure. Nothing in American cinema moved like that. The trolley ride from country to city is another: a largely stationary set with the background unspooling behind the actors, combined with rear projection unusually sophisticated for the period.

The city was a full-scale set built on the Fox lot in Westwood — an urban streetscape with a working trolley line, traffic, a lake, an amusement park. Murnau placed little people in distant shots to exaggerate scale and depth. The amusement-park sequence used over a thousand extras. The budget swelled past most films of its day, and the film underperformed commercially — part of why this kind of filmmaking died: sound was cheaper to make exciting than this kind of visual orchestration.

**The "Song of Two Humans" subtitle.** The archetypal naming — The Man, The Wife, The Woman from the City — isn't quaintness. Murnau and Mayer are signaling a fable, not a story about specific people. The opening intertitle says so outright: this song is of no place and every place. The Woman from the City is barely a character; she's a force, shot from below, framed against the moon, carrying the vampiric energy Murnau perfected in *Nosferatu*.

**The structural shape.** The film is a near-perfect arc: temptation, near-murder, reconciliation, near-tragedy, renewal — closer to a passion play or folk tale than a plot. The storm at the end mirrors and inverts the lake crossing at the beginning; The Man's near-strangling of his Wife on the boat is rhymed by his near-loss of her in the water, moving him from threat to protector while the universe nearly takes her anyway. That circularity is why it feels like a song.

**The middle section confuses people — on purpose.** Many modern viewers find the long city sequence (the haircut, the photographer, the church, the fair, the dance) meandering. It is, deliberately. Murnau is showing reconciliation as a process rather than a moment. Wandering into a stranger's wedding, where The Man breaks down, is the emotional center. The comedy of the photographer's studio and the drunken pig at the fair isn't filler — it's two people falling in love again, slowly and awkwardly, through ordinary happiness. Murnau is borrowing American romantic-comedy conventions here, which is why to us it can feel like a different film wedged into the middle.

**What 1927 audiences experienced that you don't.** The synchronized Movietone score was a sensation — most audiences had never heard a film with a locked, intentional soundtrack and embedded effects rather than a live theater organist. The bells, the trolley, the storm arriving *from the film itself* was new. They also experienced this as a visibly foreign art object — German Expressionism arriving in Hollywood, with chiaroscuro lighting, distorted city geometry, and the dream-logic of the marsh. American audiences read that as artistic or pretentious depending on temperament, which is part of why it was a critical triumph and a commercial disappointment; much of the country found it slow and strange.

**The ending and what it's doing.** The sunrise is the literal answer to the title. The film opens at dusk — the Woman from the City arriving by train, darkness, temptation — and closes at dawn; The Man has passed through the night of the soul. The Woman from the City leaves on the same tracks she came in on, defeated, but Murnau never moralizes over her or punishes her. She simply exits the fable. The moral system is older than melodrama, nearer a fairy tale or a medieval mystery play.

**The genuinely contested thing.** The near-murder is hard for modern viewers. The Man plots to drown his Wife, nearly does it, then "earns" forgiveness across a single good day. Some find this morally repugnant — should she forgive him at all? The fable register is the film's answer: this is a soul almost lost and recovered, not a realist account of a marriage. But it's a legitimate critique worth sitting with, not dismissing — Molly Haskell and Tania Modleski, among others, have written sharply about the gender politics. The film asks you to operate symbolically and forgive accordingly; whether that's earned is a fair argument.

**What to watch next.** *The Last Laugh* (1924) shows what Murnau was doing in Germany just before — the "unchained camera" originates there. *City Girl* (1930) is his quieter American masterpiece. *Tabu* (1931), made independently in the South Seas, was his last; he died days before its premiere. And note Janet Gaynor won the first Best Actress Oscar partly for this film, under the old rules that combined performances across several pictures. George O'Brien's physicality as The Man — that weighted, lumbering walk toward the boat — is one of the great silent performances and underdiscussed.
